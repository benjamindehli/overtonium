Overtonium / Controls

Controls

Thirty-two channel strips, a noise channel, and a bar of global controls across the top. Every strip carries the same twenty-three controls, which is seven hundred and thirty-six of them before the bar is counted, so a good deal of the design is about not having to move them one at a time.

What the modulators trace

Both per-partial modulators have a shape as well as a rate and a depth, picked from a small drawing of the wave between the two knobs. Click it for the list by name. Pitch offers eight, amplitude seven, and each channel has its own, the noise channel included, which is the point: the fifth partial can be gated while the fourth breathes.

Shape What it does
Sine The default, and what both modulators were before there was a choice
Triangle The same travel at an even speed, which reads as more mechanical than a sine
Sawtooth, Reverse Sawtooth A ramp and a snap back, either way round
Square Two values and nothing in between. On pitch it is a trill, and comes in a bipolar form that goes the same distance either side of the note and a unipolar one that only ever goes up
Sample & Hold A new random value each cycle, held until the next. On pitch, with the depth wide open, that is a run of random notes anywhere within an octave either side
Random The same values, glided between rather than stepped. The contour DRIFT traces, under your own rate

Amplitude has one square, not two. A tremolo only ever comes down from the fader, so there is no second direction for a unipolar shape to go in and the one square gates the whole depth. For the same reason every shape on that side is read a quarter turn ahead: a note begins at full level and dips, rather than starting half attenuated.

The square and stepped shapes do not click. The gain a partial is given already slides across each control block rather than jumping, so an edge arrives as a slew of about two thirds of a millisecond, which is short enough to read as an edge and long enough not to be heard as a tick.

LINK does not reach these. It drags a value across the series along a weighted curve and there is no weighting a list of named shapes, so the shape menu offers to set every channel at once instead.

In phase across the keyboard is the other entry in that menu, one for each of the two modulators. A modulator belongs to a note by default, so striking a chord a note at a time gives you three tremolos that each started where their own key did and breathe out of step. Ticked, the modulator belongs to the channel instead and a note arriving late joins whatever is already running, so a chord breathes as one thing however it was played. That is what an instrument with one tremolo circuit in it does, which is why Wurli ships with its tremolo shared and StyloPoly with its vibrato.

Each channel keeps its own rate and shape either way. What is shared is one circuit per channel rather than one for the instrument, so a patch built on thirty-two rates that never line up still sounds like itself with the switch on. It is part of the patch, so a preset carries it, and both switches are off in anything that does not ask.

The group's heading in the gutter lights when its modulator is shared, so the state is on the panel rather than only in the menu: PITCH MOD or AMP MOD comes up in the same accent everything else switched on here does. One mark per modulator, since all thirty-three channels answer to the one switch.

Velocity and pressure, per partial

Velocity being per partial is what lets a soft note be a different timbre rather than just a quieter one. Set the fundamental to 0% and the upper partials to 100% and the tone opens up as you play harder, which is roughly what a struck string or bar does.

Both controls run either side of zero. A positive amount means harder or heavier is louder. A negative amount inverts that, so the partial is at its loudest when you play softly. The two halves are exact mirrors, so -50% at a given velocity matches +50% at the opposite velocity. The reason to want the negative half is crossfading: give one set of partials a positive amount and another set a negative one, and the two timbres trade places across the velocity range instead of one simply fading in.

Aftertouch works the same way but adds to the fader instead of scaling it, and it ignores velocity entirely. A strip with its fader all the way down is silent until you lean on the key, and then it fades in under your finger, while a negative amount fades an open strip back out. Both channel pressure and polyphonic aftertouch are accepted and whichever is higher wins. Pressure is smoothed over about 15 ms, so seven-bit MIDI does not step the gain.

The mod wheel stands in for it by default, since most keyboards have no aftertouch at all and the wheel is the control your hand already goes to. A wheel left alone reads zero, so nothing changes for a controller that does send pressure. Settings has an "Aftertouch from" entry if you would rather have one or the other on its own.

The two-part envelope

Delay, attack, decay and sustain on the way in. Letting go of a key then runs its own little envelope rather than simply fading out: the KEY OFF rows say where the level goes when the key is released and how long it takes to get there, and only then does the release run from that level down to silence.

Above the sustain that is a release click or a bloom, the sound a damper landing or a hammer returning makes, and it can be louder than the note was while you were holding it. Drawbar Organ uses it that way: the upper drawbars jump to 55% for two milliseconds as the contacts break. Below the sustain it is the fast initial drop into a long tail that a piano or a struck bell actually has, which is probably the half that gets more use. A key-off level of zero skips the stage entirely and releases from wherever the level sat.

How fast you let the key up scales that tail, on a keyboard that can sense a release. The hardest lift leaves twice the level, the softest half of it and 64 is neutral, and it scales the level the tail would have started from, so on a patch with a key-off level of its own it moves that and on a patch without one it moves the sustain. There is no knob for it and no parameter: it belongs to the gesture rather than to the patch, the way velocity does. A partial already sounding at full has nowhere for a hard lift to go, since the envelope stops at full scale.

Most keyboards cannot sense a release at all, and on those nothing changes. They say so in two different ways, either a note-off carrying zero or a note-on of velocity zero standing in for one, and both are read as no information rather than as the softest lift there is.

The delay stage holds a partial silent before its attack begins. Staggering it across the series makes the spectrum unfold rather than arrive all at once, which is how Slow Pad and Shimmer open up. It is latched in samples at note-on, so moving the knob cannot retime a note already waiting.

STRIKE sits at the head of the section because it is about the blow rather than about the shape, and it aims that blow at the two rows under it, the way the velocity row aims it at the fader. Zero ignores the gesture. Positive means a hard note starts sooner and arrives faster, and a soft one waits and then opens slowly. Negative inverts it.

The two halves lean opposite ways on purpose, and each knob stays the limit of its own row. Velocity only ever lengthens the attack, so ATTACK goes on meaning the fastest the partial gets, and it only ever pulls the delay in, so DELAY goes on meaning the latest it arrives. Both span eight octaves at full amount, a range of 256 to 1: a partial set to 2 ms attacks in 2 ms under a hard blow and takes half a second under the softest, and a 400 ms delay stays 400 ms under the softest and comes in to under two milliseconds under the hardest. The stretched attack stops at five seconds, which is as long as the ATTACK row itself goes.

Setting it per partial is what separates it from a velocity curve over the whole instrument: give the upper partials a strike amount and leave the fundamental at zero, and a hard chord lands as one bright block while a soft one unfolds, the fundamental first and the series arriving behind it.

One shape worth knowing, because it is what a music box or a thumb piano has: no sustain at all, so the partial decays to silence while the key is still down, and then a key-off level that brings it back. Reaching zero is not the same as being finished.

Ganging the channels

One button at the top of the caption column on the left chooses what a drag in the mixer does, and it is the one control that is not on the top bar. It wears the pointer you are about to be holding rather than a word: a plain arrow, an arrow with two chain links, or an arrow with a drawn contour. Clicking it names the three, and a right-click anywhere in the mixer opens the same menu, which is where you are when you want it.

Link gangs the strips: dragging any knob moves the same knob on the others. It works relatively, applying an offset to wherever each strip already sits rather than dragging everything to one shared value, so a spectrum you have shaped by hand keeps its shape. The offset is always measured from the values captured when the drag started, so returning the knob to where you began restores the strips exactly, even if some of them hit an end stop along the way.

Which channels a drag reaches and how it is shared out are set in the same menu, under the three tools, so the tool and its settings are one click apart. With Link chosen, pointing at a knob arms every knob a drag from it would move, before you touch anything, and how brightly each one lights follows the curve.

Draw is the third. A drag across the faders sets each channel it passes over from the pointer's height, so a spectrum can be drawn in one stroke rather than one fader at a time. Holding shift does the same for as long as it is held, whatever tool is chosen, and the button shows the contour for as long as the key is down, so the panel says what a drag would do however it came to be that way.

They are one choice rather than three switches because a drag cannot be two of them at once. When they were two separate switches, LINK had to be shown as off while drawing was armed without actually being off, which is a lie the mouse-up would have exposed.

It is not Link under another name: Link shares one relative move out across the channels by a rule, and drawing sets absolute values freehand, so neither can do the other's job. Columns the pointer crosses between one moment and the next are filled in, so a fast hand leaves no gaps, and the whole stroke is a single undo. It works on the faders alone, because a fader's value is where it stands and a pointer's height means nothing to a knob.

While it is armed every fader lights, in the same accent the button is lit in, so what the next drag would reach is visible before you touch anything. That is the same preview LINK uses, since both answer the one question. The pointer becomes a pencil and LINK reads as off, without being off: only one of the two can be what the next drag does, and letting go gives LINK back.

Taper is anchored on the strip you grabbed, which always gets exactly its full share of the drag. That has to be true, since the knob under the mouse follows the mouse. From there a strip's share falls off in a straight line with distance, reaching nothing thirty-one channels away.

Where you reach in is therefore the whole control. Grab channel 1 and the mixer tilts down across its full width, from everything to nothing. Grab channel 32 and it tilts up instead. Grab channel 10 and the curve peaks under your hand and falls away on both sides, to 0.71 at channel 1 and 0.29 at channel 32. Distance is measured against the full width rather than against whichever end is nearer, so the reach stays the same wherever you grab: scaling it to the nearer end would make a grab on channel 2 drop channel 1 to nothing in a single step.

LINK gangs the 32 partials only. The noise channel has no tuning, pitch modulation or drift to gang.

Working the panel

Double-clicking a knob restores its default, which is that control's own default rather than the bottom of its range. On a fader that is 0 dB rather than silence.

Undo and redo matter here more than they would on a smaller instrument, because one LINK drag moves the same knob on all 32 channels and without a history the only way back is to reload the preset. A step is a gesture rather than a value change, so that drag comes back in one rather than thirty-two. Cmd-Z and Cmd-Shift-Z, or Ctrl with the same two keys away from a Mac, work where the host lets them through, which many do not, since a DAW usually keeps those for its own history. Both are also in the Settings menu, just under the version, which always works. Loading a session starts you with an empty history rather than with whatever the last person did.

Resting on a strip names the partial it belongs to: the harmonic number, the interval that harmonic makes with the fundamental, how many semitones above the played note the tempered position is, and how far the exact ratio sits from that in cents. It is the row that channel has in the table of the series, without leaving the mixer. The mute and solo buttons say which harmonic they would silence.

Right-clicking anywhere in the mixer opens the LINK menu, which is where your hand already is when you want it. The mute and solo buttons take the click instead and clear every mute or every solo at once.

Reading the mixer

The two readouts

Each channel carries two figures, the cents its tuning knob is worth and the level its fader is at, both drawn as seven-segment displays rather than as text: a number the instrument is reporting back should look different from a number you typed. Two of the things they show are not numbers. A partial left in equal temperament reads Et and a fader all the way down reads -inF, both dimmed, because a statement of fact is not a level. The fundamental and the octaves read a dimmed 0.0, since their just interval is the note itself.

Lamps on the rules

The rules dividing a strip into groups carry a lamp each, showing what the group under them is doing to this partial right now.

Rule Shows
PITCH MOD a needle on a track, flat to the left and sharp to the right, where modulation and drift have the partial now
ENVELOPE how far up its envelope the partial is, while the key is down
KEY OFF the same, once the key is up and the key-off stage has taken over
AMP MOD how far the tremolo has pulled the level down, so it pulses at the rate and swings further at greater depth
OUTPUT two lamps side by side, one for each row beneath it: the left is what the blow has left of the partial, the right is what the pressure on the key is adding

They read from the voice pool rather than from the knobs, so they describe a note rather than a setting and nothing pulses over silence, and they follow the loudest voice on that partial, which is the one the meter follows. The output rule carries two of them, one for each row beneath it, and each is the shape of the knob above it. VELOCITY can only take level away, so its lamp shows what the row has left: full when the blow has cost the partial nothing, dark when the row has taken all of it, and full at every velocity when the knob sits at the centre. AFTERTOUCH adds instead, so its lamp rests dark and comes up as you lean on the key, and it does not matter which way that row is set to push. The needle's scale is fixed and the same on every strip, so two channels can be compared by eye. Full deflection is 225 cents, which is a vibrato and a drift at full stretch rather than the widest the depth knob now reaches. A square set to jump an octave pegs it, which is the honest thing for a needle to do when it is off the end of its scale: scaled to the octave instead, an ordinary vibrato would not move it at all.

Meters

Each channel meters what that partial is actually putting out, on a decibel scale floored at -48 dB, so the envelope, tremolo, velocity, aftertouch and mute or solo all show at once and the spectrum can be watched evolving as a note decays. The meter fills the fader's own track rather than sitting beside it, and the cap is drawn as translucent glass so the meter reads through it. It reads the loudest instance of a partial across the sounding voices rather than the sum, so it shows the shape of the patch instead of pinning itself the moment you play a chord.

Colour and hover

Channels are coloured by interval class, in chromatic order along a narrow band from blue at the octave, through magenta and red, to yellow at the major seventh, so octaves, fifths and thirds stay visible while you scroll. The band is deliberately narrow so 32 channels read as one family rather than a rainbow, and nothing in it enters the green and cyan the global controls use. Pointing at a knob picks out its row all the way across the mixer and brackets the channel it is on, so where the two cross is the control a drag would move.

Folding the mixer down

Clicking a section heading in the caption gutter folds that group of rows away across every channel at once, and so does clicking the rule that section draws across any strip, which saves crossing to the gutter and finding your way back to the partial you were working on. Pointing at a rule gives the same hand a heading does and lights that section's caption and its disclosure mark, so what the click would fold is visible before you make it. The window loses exactly the height those rows were taking. Pitch modulation, envelope, key off, amp mod and output each fold. The tuning at the top and the faders at the bottom always stay. The heading keeps its activity lamp while folded, so a group you cannot see still reports whether it is doing anything, and the fold state is remembered with the session rather than with the patch.

The noise channel

Pinned on the right, after the series it does not belong to, is a noise channel marked NZ. It has the same envelope, tremolo, velocity, aftertouch, pan, mute, solo, level and meter as a partial, and takes part in solo alongside them. What it does not have is pitch, so the tuning, pitch modulation and drift rows are empty.

COLOUR takes the tuning row instead, tilting the noise from dark rumble at one end, through flat in the middle, to bright hiss at the other. The middle really is flat rather than merely filtered less. Each voice runs its own noise stream, so playing a chord does not layer eight copies of the identical signal.

MPE

Off by default, and under Settings. With it on, a controller that gives every note its own MIDI channel can bend and press each note separately, which on this instrument means each finger gets its own copy of all 33 aftertouch destinations. Press into one note in a chord and only that note's upper partials come in.

Slide, the forward and back axis under a finger, has a destination of its own under Settings. By default it moves that note's brightness, thinning or opening the top of the series, which is what a player's hands expect from that axis. It can be aimed at the tuning instead, so that pushing one finger forward slides that note alone between equal temperament and just intonation while the rest of the chord stays where it is. A keyboard with no slide axis sends nothing and changes nothing.

It is read as movement rather than as a position: the first value a note is given becomes that note's nought, and what reaches the sound is how far the axis has travelled from it since. Controllers disagree about what the axis is, which is why. A Seaboard reports where the finger sits and rests at the centre, while an Expressive E Osmose spends the first part of the key travel on pressure and only then starts sending slide, from the bottom of its range upward. Read as a position that second kind lurches the moment the axis engages. Read as movement both start the note on the patch and follow the finger from there.

The travel is scaled so that the longer of the two directions away from the rest is the whole of the slide, and both directions move at that one rate. A rest near the bottom of the axis leaves very little travel underneath it, and giving that sliver a scale of its own would make it a hair-trigger: pressing in would feel smooth while coming back out snapped to dark. The short side keeps the long side's sensitivity and simply reaches less far, which is honest, since there is less of it.

It is a setting rather than something permanently on because it changes what a channel number means. With it off, a channel is ignored: a key-up on channel 7 releases a note that went down on channel 1, which is what a single-channel keyboard needs. With it on, the channel is part of who the note is, so the same key can be held twice on two channels, bent in two directions, as two voices rather than one retriggering the other.

Legato

The first entry in the polyphony list, above 1 voice, and the one setting where the instrument is not polyphonic at all. One voice, and pressing a key while another is still held moves the note rather than starting it again: the envelopes carry on, so a run keeps the shape the first key gave it rather than restarting the attack under your fingers.

Letting a key go while others are still down falls back to the most recent of them, which is what makes a trill work. Only the last key coming up releases the note.

Everything set once and then left. The menu is where the instrument is set up and the panel is where the sound is made, and a control's place says which of the two it is.

Almost none of it travels with a preset, and that is a rule rather than a list that might fall behind one: how you play the instrument and what it is tuned to are properties of the session rather than of any one sound in it. The zoom, the window size and which groups of rows you have folded away are in the same position for the same reason.

The safety clip is the exception in this table, and the master fader is the exception outside it. Both used to sit with the rest on the grounds that how loud the instrument is belongs to the session, and both moved when the clipper became five shapes you choose between: the fader is the drive into that stage, so how hard a patch leans on it is part of the patch rather than part of the desk. The cost is worth knowing before you go looking for it: loading a preset now moves your output level.

Automating it from the host

Eight hundred and thirty-six parameters reach the host: thirty-four global ones, twenty-three on each of the 32 partials, eighteen on the noise channel, and six for each of the eight macros. They are listed in the order the panel reads, the globals first and then channel by channel with the noise channel last.

Every per-channel name carries its channel, so a parameter list answers both ways of looking for something. Type H7 and you have that partial, type Tune and you have that row across the whole mixer. The same names are what a screen reader reads, which is why they are words rather than numbers.

What you are after What the host calls it
The master fader Master
The seventh partial's tuning H7 Tune
The eleventh partial's fader H11 Level
The noise channel's brightness Noise Colour
How long the echo's repeats are Echo Time

The noise channel has eighteen rather than twenty-three because it has no tuning, pitch modulation, drift or start phase, and gains COLOUR in their place.

Everything in Settings is in the list too, even though no preset will ever move it. The temperament, the polyphony and the bend range are all automatable, so a piece that changes temperament partway through is something a host can do, whatever the panel thinks a setting is for.

A parameter is identified by an id of its own rather than by its position, in the Audio Unit as in the VST3. That is what lets a release add controls without breaking what you have already written: 1.6.0 put 65 new parameters among the existing ones rather than after them, and every automation lane written before it still points at the control it was pointing at.

LINK is not a parameter, since it is a tool for the mouse rather than part of the sound. A drag under it opens a gesture on every channel it reaches, writes each one, and closes them together, so what a host records is what actually moved: thirty-two lanes if the drag moved thirty-two channels.

Macros

A macro is one parameter that moves a whole row, and the reason it exists is automation. A Link drag across the tuning knobs moves 32 parameters, and a host catches only the last one you touched, so a relationship you can edit by hand cannot be recorded at all. A macro gives that relationship a single control a host can draw.

The icon of three faders on the top bar, beside the settings cog, opens them over the mixer. Each macro is one line: a colour, the row it drives, which channels of that row it reaches, how it is shared out across them, and the amount. Add a macro takes one of eight, and the cross at the end puts it back.

Eight is a pool rather than a limit you are meant to feel. A plugin declares its parameters once when it is created and neither VST3 nor AU can add one later, so making a macro takes one of eight that already exist and removing it puts it back. The panel shows the ones you have made, and your host's parameter list shows all eight whether or not you use them.

Scope is All, Odd, Even, or any one interval: choosing fifths reaches harmonics 3, 6, 12 and 24. Curve is Uniform or Taper, and a taper leans hardest on a channel you pick and fades with distance either side of it. Link's third curve, Spread, is not offered and could not be: it scatters each strip along the direction your drag gave it relative to the strip under the mouse, and a macro has neither a direction nor a strip under the mouse.

A macro offsets what the patch says rather than writing it. Nothing in your sound is overwritten, the host has one lane to draw instead of thirty-two to catch, and the knobs do not move when a macro does. That last part is normal for anything that works this way and is surprising the first time, so the controls say both at once: the ring around a knob lights to where the macro has taken the value, in the macro's colour, while the pointer on the cap stays at what the patch holds, in the channel's. The gap between them is the macro. A fader, which has no ring to spare, says it in the gutters either side of its track: two narrow columns of segments appear there, cut from the same cloth as the meter behind the fader, and light from the foot of the track up to the level it has been taken to. The glass of the cap takes a wash of the same colour.

With a macro at rest the two coincide, so a macro you have assigned but not moved looks like no macro at all.

The amount runs from -1 to +1 whatever it drives, so a macro can always push a control either way from where the patch left it, and it moves a proportion of each control's own travel rather than a number of its units. That matters most on a row like decay, which runs from a thousandth of a second to twenty: the same number of seconds added would be a twentyfold change at one end and a rounding error at the other.

A full sweep reaches the ends of the range, which means a patch already sitting near one end has a stretch of fader that does nothing in that direction. That is deliberate. One macro reaches up to 32 controls that may be set anywhere, so there is no smaller fraction of the range that would be right for all of them.

Right-click a macro's amount to bind a controller to it, the same MIDI Learn the mixer's controls use. Only the amount: the row, the scope and the curve say what a macro is rather than what it is doing. The macros icon lights whenever any are made, since a macro's work is otherwise invisible until you look at a ring.

Playing it from a controller

Right-click any control and choose MIDI Learn, then move something on your controller. A violet ring marks the control while it waits, so you can see what is listening without opening the menu again, and only ever one control carries it. Right-click the same control afterwards and the menu says which controller moves it, with the way to forget it or to bind a different one. In the mixer these items sit under the LINK settings that right-click already opens. On the bar and the noise channel they are a menu of their own, since neither is part of the series LINK gangs.

Your host almost certainly offers the same thing. What this does that a host cannot is let you make the map by right-clicking the knob itself rather than finding H17 AM Rate in a list of 836, and behave the same way wherever you open it.

Eleven controllers are refused, because something is already listening for them and losing it would be felt while playing rather than noticed afterwards. Controller 1 is the mod wheel, which can feed aftertouch. 64 is the sustain pedal. 74 is the slide axis, the one most easily bound by accident since it moves whenever a finger does on an MPE keyboard. 120 and 123 stop notes. Controllers 6, 38, 98, 99, 100 and 101 are how a keyboard tells the instrument what it is. Arming a knob and then reaching for the slide to steady a note leaves the note steadied and the knob still waiting.

It works the same with MPE switched on, which is not free: with MPE on, nearly every message belongs to the part of the instrument that reads your keyboard's layout, so a binding is looked for before that rather than after it. Otherwise it would be dead on exactly the controllers this is usually played from.

The map is saved with your project, beside the window size and the folded sections, and a preset never touches it. Which controller moves which knob describes the desk you are plugged into rather than the sound, and a patch that rearranged it would be rearranging your hardware. A binding records the parameter by name, so a later version adding controls cannot silently repoint it at something else.

Two things to expect. A control jumps to wherever the controller is standing rather than waiting to be picked up, so a knob turned to the top will take the parameter there the moment you touch it. And a controller moving a control is not something undo reverses, for the same reason automation is not.

Across the whole instrument

Character

Which oscillator every partial is, at the head of the group that says what the series is. All six are sine oscillators, and what differs is the way each circuit fails to make one. Pure is the sine nothing built out of parts produces, and is what every partial was before this existed. Bulb is a Wien bridge held steady by a lamp: no added harmonics at all, but a level that lags half a second behind every move of the pitch. It answers gestures rather than sitting there. A vibrato is heard in the level as well as in the pitch, a bend dips and then recovers as the lamp catches up, and a held note with nothing moving sounds exactly like Pure, which is what a circuit whose whole job is holding the level steady does. Rail is a phase-shift oscillator grown into its own supply rail, which hardens the tone with a third harmonic and no even ones. Diode is a triangle bent into a sine by two of them that do not match, which puts a kink in one half of the wave and a second, third and fourth harmonic on top of it. Valve is a triode biased so that one half of the wave leans over before the other, which is the only one here whose loudest addition is an octave above the partial rather than a twelfth, and is the whole of what people mean by valve warmth.

Op-amp is an amplifier that cannot move as fast as a fast note asks it to. It is the one that changes as you play: below a kilohertz nothing is being limited and the partials are untouched, and above it they lose first their corners and then everything but their slopes, which is a triangle. So the top of the series hardens as it climbs while the bottom of it stays where it was, and a chord played high is a different instrument from the same chord played low.

Because this is an additive instrument, those harmonics land among the partials you are already mixing, at exact multiples of the partial that made them. The rack's own spread, below, has moved the real partials off those exact ratios, and TUNE and STRETCH move them further, so the two beat against each other rather than adding up, which is what makes a character sound like a circuit rather than like a tone control. The harmonics are dropped as a partial climbs, so nothing folds back down: a partial near the top of the range plays a plain sine whatever is chosen.

Each character is also a rack of thirty-two rather than one oscillator used thirty-two times. Units built to the same spec do not come out the same, so every character except Pure puts each partial a couple of cents and a fraction of a decibel off what it was asked for, by an amount fixed for that character: the same rack every time you load the patch, in every host and on every machine. It is not DRIFT, which wanders. Partial 1 is the one the other thirty-one were tuned against and is left exactly where it is, so a note still lands on the key you played and choosing a character does not change how loud the patch is. It is what makes the beating above happen in a patch nobody has detuned, and it is the difference between a rack of thirty-two oscillators and one oscillator copied thirty-two times. No two of them distort by quite the same amount either: each unit's circuit is built to one of three drives, fifteen percent either side of the nominal.

The button lights in the character's own colour, borrowed from the same band the channel strips take theirs from: yellow for Bulb, running down through the warm end to the red of channel 3 for Op-amp. Pure does not light, which is the honest thing for the one that adds nothing.

It is part of the patch rather than part of the setup, so a preset can change it and saving one carries it. Twenty-five of the thirty-one factory presets ask for one, so most of what you load is already on a circuit rather than on the plain sine.

Wobble

A warped record under the whole instrument. Pitch is bent by reading the output back through a delay line whose length keeps moving, which is what happens when a platter runs eccentric or a capstan slips. A slow warp near once round a 33 rpm disc, a faster wander on top, and now and then a sharp slip that bends hard and settles back. The slips grow in both rate and size with the square of the control, so a low setting is a tired turntable and a high one is a transport falling over itself. It sits ahead of the echo, so the repeats inherit what it did rather than wobbling on their own.

Bus drive

The thirty-three channels are not simply added together. They are summed onto a bus that drives, by an amount and in a way that belongs to the oscillator character, so a patch gets louder into the same saturation a mixing desk would give it. It answers to level rather than to a setting: play quietly and it is barely there, lean on the keyboard and it arrives. Pure sums cleanly and does nothing, which is what Pure is for. There is no knob, because the amount is part of what choosing a character means.

Echo

Three machines behind one button, and the button says which one is running. Tape is a loop with two motors: moving the time knob winds the tape rather than cutting to the new setting, so the repeats slide in pitch on the way, and the two paths run at slightly different speeds so a repeat comes back doubled rather than bounced. BBD is a line of buckets, which loses its top end because a bucket brigade is a sampler with no filter worth the name, and sweeps as the clock wanders. Digital is the one that does not pretend: what goes in comes back out, later and quieter, crossing to the other side on every repeat, which makes it the only ping-pong of the three.

AGE means something different on each, which is the point of having three. On the tape it is wear: the top end lost on every pass, how far the motor wanders, and how hard the tape leans over. On the BBD it is the clock slowing and the companding breathing. On the digital it is bits and sample rate, so a tail starts as the signal and ends as a memory of it. Nothing on the tape ever reaches zero wear, because a transport that held speed exactly would put the repeat back in mono and there is no such transport.

Reverb

Three machines again, on the same kind of button. Room is a feedback delay network whose room is sized from its decay time, since a long tail in a small room is a spring rather than a place. Modulated plate is a sheet of steel with no geometry to hear, so a hit spreads across all of it at once and the tail is dense from its first instant rather than arriving in stages. Its allpasses wander further than a real plate's would, which is why the menu says modulated: without that, a long decay repeats its own pattern instead of washing. Spring is a tray of helical springs, and what makes it one is that a helix carries the top of the band faster than the bottom, so a hit comes back as a chirp that starts high and falls. Every bounce adds another, which is why a spring gets less like what you played as it decays rather than merely quieter.

DECAY, DAMP and PRE mean the same thing on all three and are matched, so switching machines changes the character of a tail and not its length or its loudness. DAMP is the sheet losing its top end to the air on the plate and the spring's own loss on every crossing of the tray.

The converter

Shown as two seven-segment readouts under the output meter. Turning the sample rate down is a genuine cut rather than a filter over the top: the whole voice pool renders slower, which costs a fifth as much at 8 kHz, and the partials above the new ceiling fold back down instead of disappearing.

CLIP

Five machines behind one button, across the finished output and after the master fader, which is therefore the drive into them. The fader and the choice both travel with the preset, so how hard a patch leans on this stage is part of the patch. Soft runs straight to seven tenths and then bends, gradually and symmetrically, and is what every preset was dialled against. Hard is a wall at full scale: nothing happens until it does, which is the sound of a converter with no headroom left. Asymmetric lets one half of the wave lean over before the other, the way a single-ended stage does, and that lopsidedness is the whole of it: where Soft and Hard treat both halves alike and give odd harmonics, this gives even ones, which is a different kind of loud rather than more of it. Limiter does not bend the wave at all, it turns the level down as the signal approaches the ceiling and lets it back up afterwards. Fold turns the wave back on itself instead of flattening it, which makes far more harmonics than the others and moves them with the level, so it sings where the rest crunch.

Off lets the output go past full scale and reach the host as it is, which is what you want when something downstream is doing the catching. None of the five will: being bounded by full scale is the point of the stage rather than a property of the shapes chosen, and each is held to it however hard it is driven. The button abbreviates Asymmetric to ASYM and Limiter to LIMIT, for room, while the menu and your host's automation lane spell them out. It stands under the meter with the converter readouts because those three are the whole of what happens to the signal on its way out, and the switch is also in the Settings menu.

The Limiter looks two milliseconds ahead, so the gain is already in place when the peak arrives rather than chasing it afterwards, which is the difference between a limiter and a clipper wearing the name. That costs latency, and because a plugin whose latency moved when you chose a preset would have the host re-plan its graph every time, it is paid by all five shapes and by the stage switched off alike. Overtonium reports 108 samples at 48 kHz, a little over two milliseconds, which your host compensates for on playback.

Limiter lookahead in the Settings menu gives those two milliseconds back. Only the Limiter uses the window, so if you never reach for it you are paying for nothing, and this is the way to stop. Turning it off does not break the limiter: the detector sees a single sample instead of a window, so the gain is exact and immediate and the ceiling still holds, but the smoothing goes with it and a gain that steps is itself a distortion, about fifteen times as much of it. It is in Settings rather than on the bar because nothing a preset can reach is allowed to change what the plugin tells your host. Most hosts take the new figure while running, some only on playback stop or on reload, so it is worth choosing before a take rather than during one.

The output meter

Horizontal, split into left and right, because the two channels are identical until something is panned off centre and a summed meter would hide precisely that. It reads the finished output after master gain and the clipper. Lamps run from teal through amber to orange as they approach full scale, anchored to their decibel positions rather than stretching with the level, with a scale beneath marked at -48, -36, -24, -12, -6 and 0 dB. Each bar carries a peak hold for a couple of seconds.
